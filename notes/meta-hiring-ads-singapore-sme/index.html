What Singapore SMEs should know before running Meta hiring ads

Facebook and Instagram can reach people who are not on job portals. Here is what to get right first: Meta's rules, fair wording, the ad itself, and how you reply.

28 October 2026 · By Hunter Foo

Facebook and Instagram are where a lot of people scroll after work, on the MRT, or during lunch. A hiring ad there can reach people who are not checking job portals that week. It can also burn money fast if you set it up like a product ad. Here is what we tell Singapore SMEs before they spend on Meta hiring ads.

1. Meta treats job ads as their own category

Meta counts an ad as an employment ad when it promotes or links to a job opportunity: full-time or part-time roles, internships, job fairs, or company perks shown alongside openings. General career advice does not count.

For employment ads Meta has a Special Ad Category called Employment. You must choose it if you are an advertiser based in the US, or if your ads reach people in the US, Canada or a list of European countries. When it applies, targeting is limited:

  • Age is fixed at 18 to 65+, and you cannot pick a gender.
  • No postcode targeting and no location exclusions. Targeting a city, address or pin widens to a minimum radius (25 km in the US and Canada, 15 km in Europe).
  • No demographic or behaviour targeting, only a limited set of approved interests, and no lookalike audiences.

If you are a Singapore business and your ads only reach Singapore or Malaysia, Meta does not currently force these restrictions on you, but Ads Manager may still ask whether your campaign is an employment ad. Meta changes these rules from time to time, so check its help page, About ads for employment, on the day you set up the campaign.

2. Do not use targeting to filter people out

Even where the Employment category is not mandatory, avoid narrowing your audience by age or gender to screen candidates. Meta's advertising policies do not allow discriminatory ads, and in Singapore the Tripartite Guidelines on Fair Employment Practices ask employers to select on skills, experience and qualifications.

The same applies to your wording. TAFEP's guide to writing fair job advertisements lists phrases to avoid: age ranges such as "below 30 only", "preferably female", race or religion preferences, and nationality preferences, including lines like "Malaysians welcome". Language requirements need a job-related reason.

What works better is a reasonable location (where people can realistically commute from) and an ad that does the filtering for you by being specific about the job.

3. Write the ad like a job, not a slogan

  • First line: job title and area. "Warehouse assistant, Tuas" beats "Join our growing family!"
  • The facts people decide on: pay range if you can share it, working days and shift hours, and three or four real requirements.
  • What happens next: "Message us on WhatsApp, we reply within the day" tells people exactly what to do.
  • A real picture: the actual workplace, the team at work, or a clean text card with the job title. Stock photos of a smiling office team for a warehouse role attract the wrong people.
  • No overpromising: "easy money" or "high pay, no experience" invites rejection and people who drop out at the interview stage.

Keep the caption short and easy to scan. If many of your applicants read Chinese or Malay, a separate ad in that language usually reads better than one long mixed caption.

4. Let applicants reach you on WhatsApp

Most job seekers here would rather send a WhatsApp message than fill in a long form. Meta lets you run ads that open a WhatsApp chat in one tap. Before the ad goes live, prepare:

  • A greeting that asks three or four simple questions: which job, which area they live in, when they can start, and their relevant experience.
  • Quick replies for the questions you get every day: pay, hours, location, transport.
  • Labels in WhatsApp Business (new, screened, interview booked, hired, not suitable) so nobody falls through the cracks.

Do not ask for full NRIC numbers over chat. Singapore's PDPC has advisory guidelines on NRIC numbers that limit when organisations may collect them.

5. Speed of reply matters more than the ad

People who reply to a hiring ad on social media are often messaging several employers at the same time. The ad gets you the conversation; your reply decides whether they show up.

  • Name one person who owns the inbox while the ad is running, and give them fixed times in the day to clear it.
  • Offer two or three interview slots instead of asking "when are you free?"
  • Send a reminder the day before and on the morning of the interview.
  • When the role is filled, tell the people still waiting. It keeps your page's name clean for the next round.

6. Measure conversations and interviews, not likes

A hiring ad with many likes and few messages has not done its job. Track how many people messaged, how many passed your basic questions, how many attended an interview, and how many started work. Start with a modest budget, change one thing at a time (the image, the first line, the location), and only spend more once people are actually turning up.
